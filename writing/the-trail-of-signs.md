---
title: The Trail of Signs that Never Ends in *The Crying of Lot 49*
date: 2026-11-14
---

In Pynchon’s *The Crying of Lot 49*, Oedipa Maas expects signs to behave as signs should. They should point beyond themselves and unfold into a meaning she can take as an answer. They do not. Pierce Inverarity is absent, and he leaves behind a trail of signs. In Saussurean terms, each sign is defined only by its relation to other signs. Meaning is therefore never delivered, only deferred, and the chain broadens rather than narrows. The only meaning available is the one a person projects onto the trail. <mark>The only way out of the deferral is to settle: to accept that projected, partial meaning as enough.</mark>

The novel suggests this is what a finite life requires. Oedipa will not settle, and Pynchon’s skeletal characters show why. Each is a surface that points onward, not to an interior where the search could stop. Driblette, the author of the play, should be the end of the trail, but he is consumed by the system he made. The dying sailor is the one figure whose actions are not meant to be interpreted by Oedipa. He is the only one who interrupts her journey for a very brief time. She tries to investigate him as she does everything else, and she fails. He gives her nothing to resolve, and resolution is all she wants. The novel thus makes endless deferral a question of what it takes to be satisfied without an answer. Its unresolved auction leaves Oedipa, and the reader, unwilling or unable to settle.

Even in the few scenes where Pierce’s life is remembered, Pynchon does not characterize him as an anchor, foreshadowing that there truly is no termination to his signs. Pierce Inverarity’s entrance is when he is already dead. A law firm’s letter says he “had died back in the spring” and names Oedipa executor, “or she supposed executrix,” of an estate “numerous and tangled enough to make the job of sorting it all out more than honorary.” Her first response is to read. Looking over the letter and what he left, she wonders whether he died “crushed by the only ikon in the house,” a bust of Jay Gould. A man is dead, and she is already reading into it. Pierce’s absence immediately produces some interpretation before there is actually anything to interpret. Later, she recounts a conversation she had with Pierce, where he spoke in a string of voices, and she will never know where it came from “(unless now he’d left a diary).” Additionally, the parentheses show what she expects from Pierce’s estate and will: an answer.

Pynchon’s other characters are built as flat surfaces that tend to point onwards, and never inwards. Their unusual names and even more unusual actions send Oedipa from sign to sign. A novel with characters named Mike Fallopian, Dr. Hilarius and Genghis Cohen is not hiding the joke. None of them give her any space to stop and reflect. Mike Fallopian, whose name already suggests a passage rather than a destination, behaves like one. The founding story of the Peter Pinguid Society has many holes: the ship was “either the corvette ‘Bogatir’ or the clipper ‘Gaidamak,’” the place “either what is now Carmel-by-the-Sea, or what is now Pismo Beach.” But that does not matter to Fallopian. “We don’t try to make scripture out of it,” he says. The story matters only for its ripples, which “spread, and grew, and today engulf us all,” a history that broadens rather than closes.

Their mail system works the same way. Members have to send a letter every week, which leads to brief, empty letters and further disorder. This also parallels the novel itself, which constantly gets more chaotic for Oedipa, and each sign leaves her with more questions than answers. Her memory of Fallopian is just as hollow. She keeps only:

> his slender build and neat Armenian nose, and a certain affinity of his eyes for green neon

The whimsiness of this description lies in Pynchon’s odd precision. He notes the nose is Armenian, but does not reveal anything else about Fallopian. Even his eyes are described outwards, constantly searching for neon light. The description is both funny and complete, since there is nothing more for the reader to know about Fallopian; he is just a sign for Oedipa to follow.

Driblette is the one figure who claims to stand at the end of the trail. When asked for the original text, he tells Oedipa he only has damaged copies and sends her to another paperback, because the words, he insists, do not matter:

> the reality is in this head. Mine. I’m the projector at the planetarium

This is the strongest case for Driblette knowing the end of the trail, since “projector” seems to imply he can control how the clues end up in the real world and in what format. But, unfortunately for Oedipa, Driblette exposes his own limit. If he disappeared, his control over the play would disappear. Which is exactly what happens when he walks into the ocean and becomes “traces, fossils.” The author is dead, and all that remains are still signs with no one behind them. Oedipa’s response shows how little this changes her. She no longer asks why but waits “as if to be illuminated,” mourns him as her “best guide back to the Trystero,” and within moments is asking Bortz about the paperback.

The dying sailor is where Oedipa’s investigation fails. She approaches him because of a sign, “fascinated” about the post horn on his hand, but his face stops her, and her first words are not about the tattoo, but “Can I help?”, a reaction no other character in the novel has elicited out of her. What follows is the novel’s only scene of real contact, and Pynchon depicts it as her needing to touch the sailor “as if she could not believe in him, or would not remember him, without it.” This suddenly interrupts her investigation, grounding her back into the world, devoid of these obscure signs. But even holding him, she knows the limit of this contact, and the limit of not being able to accept a partial answer. She whispers, twice:

> I can’t help.

Here, Pynchon emphasizes two meanings; she can neither stay and help the sailor, nor can she accept his partial answer as a final answer. Once in his room, she starts calculating and investigating again. She “ran through [...] a scene she might play”: court, a new suit, bus fare to Fresno. It depicts her as a director like Driblette, staging a rescue in her head. While she projects, the sailor releases her hand. She does not feel it go, since she is too caught up in trying to understand the signs. She then tries to read him, turning his DT’s into a calculus pun and his mattress into a lesson in entropy, and the reading reaches its own limit: “nothing she knew of would preserve them, or him.” While the sailor provides a momentary relief for Oedipa, she eventually subconsciously starts investigating and reading him, denying the only escape that the novel provides her.

The last sentence of the novel also gives her a sense of safety, though Pynchon cleverly highlights that it is really a false sense of safety.

> Oedipa settled back, to await the crying of lot 49.

By, at last, settling, Pynchon grants Oedipa what this essay has argued that she refuses to do. But the sentence also divides the word into distinct meanings. She settles back physically, but not mentally, since she must now “await” the crying of the lot. Her expectation does not settle, as though she had reached the end of the trail, but Pynchon ends the novel before the final sign is revealed. By ending the novel, Pynchon emphasizes this cycle of endless deferral, suggesting that both Oedipa and the reader should have settled for something earlier.

*The Crying of Lot 49* does not withhold its answer only to frustrate Oedipa. It withholds it to catch the reader in her madness. We decode the names and follow the clues, and our disappointment at the last page shows that we never settled either. I am no better. This essay is one more attempt to make the trail end somewhere. Driblette had already named the trap: one could “put together clues, develop a thesis” but “never touch the truth.” Instead of the answer, the novel offers a partial meaning. <mark>Settling for it would not solve the novel, but it would mean no longer needing the novel solved</mark>, which may be the most human thing Pynchon asks of his reader, and the one thing Oedipa cannot do.
